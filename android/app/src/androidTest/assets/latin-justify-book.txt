The lamplighter walked the length of the harbour road every evening, and every evening the same gulls wheeled above the same grey water, complaining about the same indifferent weather. He had counted the lamps once, when he was young enough to think counting them mattered, and had arrived at a number he no longer trusted. There were more lamps now, or fewer, depending on which winter you asked about, and the road itself had been widened twice since the counting, so the number belonged to a road that no longer existed anywhere except in the habit of his walking.
What interested him was not the lamps but the intervals between them, the stretches of unlit pavement where the light from one post had given up and the light from the next had not yet taken hold. In those intervals the harbour looked older than it was, and the boats at their moorings became shapes rather than boats, and a person walking towards him remained a rumour of a person for four or five seconds longer than seemed reasonable. He had come to think of those seconds as the honest part of the evening, the part that did not pretend to know anything at all.
Justification is a typographic operation that distributes the slack of a line into the spaces between its words, so that the right edge of a paragraph arrives flush against the measure instead of trailing off in a ragged fringe. It is an old convention and a quiet one: nobody notices a justified column until they see an unjustified one beside it, at which point the ragged edge looks less like a choice and more like an oversight. The operation depends entirely on there being spaces to stretch, a condition ordinary prose in this script satisfies without ever being asked.
By the time he reached the end of the road the lamps behind him had settled into their steady yellow and the ones ahead were still deciding. He turned around, as he always did, and walked back through the intervals in the opposite order, which made them different intervals entirely, and arrived home with the distinct impression of having taken two separate walks. The gulls, who had been there for the whole of it, offered no opinion on the matter and went on complaining about the weather until it was fully dark.
The harbour master kept a ledger of arrivals and departures in a hand so small that reading it required the same lamp the lamplighter had lit an hour earlier, and so the two men were, in a sense, collaborators, though neither had ever said so aloud. The ledger recorded weather as a single word per day, which the lamplighter considered an act of extraordinary editorial restraint given how much the gulls found to say about it.
